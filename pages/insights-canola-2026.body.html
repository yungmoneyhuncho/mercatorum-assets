Canola outlook: tight stocks, soft demand, a curious basis

Ending stocks are below the five-year average. Crushers aren't paying up. The basis is doing something it shouldn't. A read on what we think happens next.

12 May 2026 By the Mercatorum desk 7-minute read

Canada will end the 2025/26 crop year with canola stocks around the low end of the five-year band. By the standard logic, that should be showing up in the basis — buyers paying over the futures to lock in physical product. It isn't. Track basis at a Saskatchewan elevator over the past eight weeks and you'll see it drift sideways, even soften at the margins.

The reason isn't mysterious. Two things are pulling in opposite directions: a tight carry-out on the supply side, and unusually cautious behaviour by domestic crushers and export-program buyers on the demand side. We think the demand side is the temporary one.

The basis tells the real story

Futures are doing what futures do — pricing the next twelve months of weather, planted area, and macro flows. The basis tells you what's happening on the physical side this week. Right now the cash market is acting like there's plenty of canola available. Statistics Canada says otherwise. So either the data is wrong, or the buyers know something the data doesn't.

Crush margins falling alongside a softer basis isn't unusual — but softening basis with sub-average stocks is. The cleanest explanation: the major crushers are well-covered into July, and the export program is being slow-walked while currency and freight settle.

"It's not a story about the crop. It's a story about who isn't buying — and that doesn't last forever."

What changes the picture

  1. Crush coverage rolls off. Domestic crush plants are running on tonnes booked in February. Once that book empties — probably late June — they're back in the market and the basis snaps.
  2. European rapeseed. The EU is short and is rotating through Australian and Canadian product. Watch the FOB Vancouver to CIF Rotterdam spread; if it compresses, expect exporters to step up.
  3. The biodiesel pull. Renewable-fuel mandates in the US continue to bite. Soy oil sets the ceiling, but canola oil follows it within a few weeks.

Where prices likely settle

Our base case for July is that cash levels in the major elevator catchments lift CAD $20–35/t off current levels. That's not a call on the futures — that's a call on the basis specifically. If futures hold the recent range, growers should see firmer farm-gate prices than the cash board is suggesting today.

We'd be wrong if: (a) the export program collapses for currency reasons, (b) the US biodiesel pull weakens unexpectedly, or (c) StatCan revises the carry-out upward — none of which we'd assign a high probability to right now.

What we're doing

For producers under our contract program, we're encouraging staggered pricing through July rather than waiting on a single fill. For importers, current offers out of Vancouver into Northeast Asia are reasonable but unlikely to be the floor. We're quoting two-month windows rather than spot.
