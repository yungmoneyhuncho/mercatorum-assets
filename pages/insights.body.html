Energy · 21 Apr 2026 · 4-minute read

Propane is quietly the best-paid molecule in Western Canada

Everyone with a screen open is watching the WCS differential — fair enough, it's the headline number for Canadian heavy crude. But for the last two quarters the molecule actually paying the bills out of the fractionators has been propane, and to a lesser extent butane.

The reason is the arb to Asia. With West Coast LPG export capacity expanded, Western Canadian propane now has a clean, short-haul route to buyers in Japan, Korea, and increasingly China. That has pulled the local price off its historic Mont Belvieu discount and handed producers a genuine netback to tidewater for the first time in years.

For term buyers, two things follow. First, the days of scooping up cheap distressed Edmonton propane in shoulder season are mostly over — the export pull smooths the seasonal trough. Second, the spread between a rail-delivered domestic contract and an FOB-terminal cargo is now wide enough that it's worth modelling both structures before you commit.

We're seeing the firmest interest from petrochemical and heating buyers who want a year-round baseload with optionality on the winter peak. If that's you, the conversation is worth having before the next contracting round.

Chemicals · 14 Apr 2026 · 4-minute read

Potash settles, urea doesn't: a tale of two fertilizers

Two of the biggest lines we move — potash and urea — are behaving like they live in different worlds right now, and for buyers planning the spring application season that divergence matters.

Potash has found a floor. Saskatchewan tonnes are contracting into India and China at numbers that, while well off the 2022 spike, are stable enough to plan around. The supply side is concentrated and disciplined, the reserve base is enormous, and nobody benefits from a price war. We'd treat the current band as a reasonable planning assumption into the back half of the year.

Urea is the opposite story. Nitrogen is still whipping around on two inputs that don't care about your planting calendar: European gas and Chinese export policy. When Beijing tightens the export taps to protect domestic supply, the world's swing tonnes vanish and the price gaps higher; when the taps open, it slides. Add a cold snap on the gas side and you can move fifty dollars a tonne in a week.

The practical takeaway: if you need urea, be ready to move on a window rather than waiting for a "settled" number that may never come. If you need potash, you have more room to be patient. Either way — tell us the tonnage and the destination and we'll show you where the current offers actually sit.

Energy · 7 Apr 2026 · 3-minute read

WCS is wide again — what it means if you buy Canadian heavy

The WCS-to-WTI differential pushed back out past the US$15 mark this month, and the inbox filled with the usual question: is something wrong with Canadian crude? Short answer — no. This is a plumbing story, not a demand story.

Two things converged: spring turnaround season at several US refineries that run heavy Canadian barrels, and pipeline apportionment that backed barrels up at Hardisty. When takeaway tightens and a chunk of refining demand goes offline at the same time, the differential is the pressure-release valve. None of it says anything about the underlying appetite for heavy sour.

For a refiner configured to run Canadian heavy, a wide diff is the opposite of a warning — it's margin. The barrels that clear at a discount today are the same quality they were last quarter. The read we're giving term buyers is to treat the current band as a buying window and lock structure before turnarounds wrap and the diff snaps back in.

Grain & oilseed · 31 Mar 2026 · 3-minute read

Durum's quiet premium: why the spread to milling wheat is holding

Durum doesn't get the headlines that canola or wheat do, but the premium it carries over ordinary milling wheat has been stubbornly fat — and it's worth understanding why before you build a procurement plan around it.

The global durum balance is thin. It's a niche crop grown in a handful of regions, and Canadian supply out of Saskatchewan and Alberta is a big share of the world's tradeable tonnes. When North African and Italian pasta demand stays firm and any one origin has a weather scare, the premium doesn't have much cushion to give back.

For growers, that premium is real money per acre and worth capturing on a scale-up rather than all at once. For buyers around the Mediterranean and beyond, the practical move is to secure quality durum early rather than chase the bottom — the discounts that show up in a thin market tend to be quality-driven, not bargains.

Pulses · 24 Mar 2026 · 3-minute read

Yellow peas: the protein bid that won't quit

Yellow peas used to be a simple story — bulk into the Indian dal market and call it a day. Now they're being pulled in two directions at once, and that tension is shaping where Canadian peas actually go.

On one side is traditional food demand: split peas and flour into South Asia and the Middle East, sensitive to India's tariff posture and its own harvest. On the other is the plant-protein industry, which fractionates yellow peas into isolate for everything from meat alternatives to sports nutrition — a steadier, premium pull that doesn't care about the monsoon.

The result is a floor under the market that wasn't there a decade ago. When the food bid softens, the protein bid quietly absorbs tonnes; when both are on at once, Canadian peas get tight fast. We'd plan around firmness rather than weakness into the back half of the year.

Macro · 17 Mar 2026 · 3-minute read

A softer loonie is a tailwind on every Canadian cargo

It's the least glamorous line on any of our desks, but the Canada–US exchange rate quietly moves more margin than most commodity calls. Nearly everything we sell prices in US dollars; a big share of what it costs to grow, refine, and move it sits in Canadian dollars. When the loonie softens, Canadian origin gets more competitive without anyone changing a single price.

That's the tailwind right now. A weaker CAD widens the gap between a Canadian offer and a US Gulf or Black Sea alternative on grain, pulses, and even some energy and fertilizer lanes. For an importer comparing origins, it can be the difference that makes a Canadian cargo the cheapest landed option.

The caution is the obvious one: FX cuts both ways and doesn't sit still. We don't advise building a multi-year plan on a currency level. But for buyers with flexibility on timing, a soft loonie is a reason to look hard at Canadian supply now rather than later.
